Audit rules 
Key idea: an audit compares what a system should do with what its records show it does, and writes 
down the gaps with evidence. You do it on paper, from the audit file; nothing is tested live. 
Before the audit starts, everyone reads and acknowledges these rules: signed on paper face-to-face, 
ticked in the submission form online. 
1. Work only from the Module 4 audit file. Everything in it is fictional. 
2. Do not scan, probe or sign in to any real system, including your own office's. Accessing a 
computer system without right is illegal access under the Cybercrime Prevention Act (RA 10175, 
Section 4(a)(1)), so scope is a legal line, not a courtesy. 
3. Never add real names, accounts or data to your findings. 
4. Work on your own. You may ask the facilitator about the rules or the records, not for answers. 
5. Submit through the submission link by 4:30 PM. 
The same habit protects your office: only people with written authority test a system, and only within 
the scope they were given.

When a device is lost 
A lost, unencrypted laptop holding sensitive personal information can be a notifiable breach under the 
3S test from Module 1: sensitive data, reason to believe an unauthorized person has it, and a real risk of 
serious harm (NPC Circular 16-03, Section 11). Report a lost device at once, because the 72-hour clock 
may already be running. Encryption is what turns "we lost the data" into "we lost a device".

Lesson 4.2: Safe websites and approved tools 
Key idea: the padlock means private, not honest, and office work uses approved tools only.

Criminals can get a certificate for an address they control. In June 2019 the FBI's Internet Crime 
Complaint Center warned that HTTPS and the padlock do not prove a site is legitimate, and that phishing 
sites were using them to look safe (Infosecurity Magazine). 
Certificate warnings 
A full-page warning such as "Your connection is not private" means the browser could not confirm the 
site's identity. Do not click past it and do not sign in. If it appears on an office system, tell IT: the usual 
cause is an expired or wrongly set-up certificate, which IT must fix rather than staff learning to ignore.

message. A password manager also helps: it offers your password only on the address where you saved

Downloads and pop-ups 
• 
A page that says your browser is out of date and offers a download: close the tab. Updates come 
from the browser or the operating system itself, or from IT. 
• 
A pop-up that says your computer is infected and gives a number to call: never call it. Close the 
browser and tell IT. 
• 
An unexpected download: do not open it. Report it. 
Shadow IT: the tool nobody approved 
Shadow IT is any app, website, account or device used for office work without approval: a free file 
converter, a personal cloud drive, a personal messaging group, a browser extension, a public AI chatbot. 
Why it matters even when nothing leaks: 
• 
The office stays responsible. Under the Data Privacy Act, a personal information controller 
remains responsible for personal data it hands to another party for processing, and must use 
contracts or other reasonable means to keep it protected (RA 10173, Sections 14 and 21(a)). A 
free website has no agreement with the RSSO. 
• 
No controls and no record. The office cannot see who opened the file, where it is stored, or 
whether it can be deleted. 
• 
No way back. Once uploaded, the office cannot recall the data. 
The approved-tools route 
1. Ask first: is there an approved tool for this already? 
2. Request it: if not, tell IT what you need to do and what data is involved. 
3. Check it: IT checks security; the DPO checks privacy, with a privacy impact assessment where 
personal data is involved. 
4. Agree terms: if the vendor will process personal data, a written agreement comes first. 
5. List it: the tool goes on the approved list, with who may use it and for what data. 
A fast route is itself a control: people take workarounds when the official way is slow. 
What IT can add 
• 
Web filtering blocks known malicious and look-alike sites (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A 8.23). 
• 
Data leakage prevention warns or blocks when sensitive data leaves by email, upload or USB 
(8.12). 
• 
Supplier controls put security duties into contracts and check cloud services before use (5.19 to 
5.23). 
• 
Buying a new web system: ask the vendor for proof that it was security-tested before go-live, 
for example against the OWASP Top 10, and who will fix problems found later.

The test: does it change a decision this week? 
Before you forward an item, ask whether it changes something you will do this week: an update to 
install, a warning to give staff, an account to check, a rule to change. If yes, act and tell the people who 
must act. If no, file it for the monthly bulletin or let it go. Forwarding everything trains people to ignore 
warnings.

Department awareness that lasts 
ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A 6.3 (Information security awareness, education and training) and NPC 
Circular 16-03, Section 6, both call for training. Habits last when they are short, frequent and local: 
• 
A one-page monthly bulletin: one threat, one habit, one contact. 
• 
Onboarding: a security and privacy briefing before anyone receives system access. 
• 
Focal persons: one per unit, who passes warnings on and collects questions. 
• 
Easy reporting: one email address or one number for anything suspicious, and thanks for every 
report. 
• 
Practice: short phishing simulations, approved by management in advance, followed by 
teaching, never shaming. 
• 
Measure what matters: the report rate (how many people report a suspicious message) should 
rise, and the click rate should fall.
