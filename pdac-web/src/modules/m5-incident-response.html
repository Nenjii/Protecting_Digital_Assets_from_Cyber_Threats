Six phases, from prepare to learn

Prepare

Are we ready before anything happens? A signed plan, a named team, backups restored and tested.

Detect

Is it real, how bad, and is personal data involved?

Contain

Stop the spread without destroying evidence.

Eradicate

Remove the cause; close the way in.

Recover

Return to service safely, from backups verified clean.

Learn

What do we change so it does not happen again? Fix the Module 4 findings.

Other frameworks, same ideas

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3 (April 2025) places incident response inside the six functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0: Govern, Identify and Protect prepare for incidents; Detect, Respond and Recover handle them; lessons learned feed improvement. DICT has said it plans a six-stage model under the national cybersecurity plan: Identify, Contain, Analyze, Eradicate, Recover, and Lesson Learned.

TIME: 3 minutes.

SAY: the four shaded phases are the response itself; preparing comes before and learning after. Learning feeds the next round of preparation: each incident should leave the office better prepared than before. The memory phrase is the whole row: prepare, detect, contain, eradicate, recover, learn.

PLAIN WORDS: containment stops an incident from spreading; eradication removes the cause, such as malware or the attacker's access; recovery returns systems to service safely.

Stop the spread, keep the evidence

Isolate first, power off last

Disconnect from the network; switch off only if you cannot disconnect, because powering down can destroy memory evidence.

Preserve before you wipe

Copy the logs and image affected systems before anything is cleaned.

Log it

Every action and decision into one incident log: who, what, when, why.

Talk on a separate channel

Phones or in person, until IT confirms the attacker cannot read office email.

Restore only from clean backups

A backup taken after the attacker got in may bring them back.

Keep a chain of custody

For every copy or device kept: what, who, when, where, and who has handled it since.

TIME: 3 minutes.

SAY: the two highlighted habits save the most marks and the most cases: isolate first, power off last, and preserve before you wipe. These come straight to your desk card.

Paying does not end the duty to notify

  • The FBI does not support paying: it does not guarantee the data back, and it encourages more attacks.
  • The data has already been taken, so the duty to notify remains.
  • The decision belongs to the head of office, with legal advice and law enforcement involved — and it is recorded in the log, with the reasons.

TIME: 2 minutes.

SAY: in the 2023 ransomware case from Module 1, the office said it would not pay. In the capstone, someone will pressure you to pay and to say nothing — have your reasons ready, and put the decision and its reasons in the log.

A ransom note. What do you do first?

Put these in a safe order, and cross out any that do not belong in the first hour

  1. Restore the server from last night's backup
  2. Disconnect the affected server from the network
  3. Open the incident log and write down what Ben saw and when
  4. Tell the DPO that applicant data may be involved
  5. Wipe the infected computers to stop the spread
  6. Copy the logs and image affected systems before anything changes
  7. Declare the incident and its severity
  8. Post on the office's page that everything is fine
  1. 7 and 3 together — declare the incident and its severity, and open the log.
  2. 2 — disconnect the server (do not switch it off).
  3. 6 — preserve the logs and images.
  4. 4 — tell the DPO that applicant data may be involved.

Not in the first hour: 1, a restore comes only after eradication, from a backup verified clean. 5, wiping before preserving destroys evidence, and clean-up belongs to eradication. 8, never — a false statement misleads the public, and hiding a breach of sensitive personal information is a crime under the Data Privacy Act, Section 30. Use a holding statement instead.

Must we notify? Smoke, not ashes

TIME: 3 minutes.

SAY: you call the fire department when you smell smoke, not when you see ashes. Notify the NPC and the people affected when all three are true: Sensitive, Stolen, Serious. A single no means no notification is required, but you still document the decision. Three yeses start the 72-hour duty. When unsure, weigh the likelihood of harm first — and whether 100 or more people are involved, public safety, confidential information, or vulnerable groups.

ON SCREEN: step through the three questions; A shows the outcome; R resets.

72 hours to notify, 5 days to report

TIME: 2 minutes.

SAY: certainty is not required — notify on what you have and send more as you learn it. The NPC and each affected person within 72 hours of knowing or reasonably believing; the full report within 5 days of discovery. When 100 or more people are affected, no delay is allowed. File through the NPC's DBNMS.

What the notices must say

To the NPC (Sec. 17)

  • How it happened and the weakness that allowed it; a timeline
  • The approximate number of people or records, and the kind of breach
  • The sensitive data involved, and anything that could enable identity fraud
  • What was done and is planned; how people are being told; the DPO's contact

To each affected person (Sec. 18)

  • What happened, and which of their data may be involved
  • What the office has done to address it and to reduce harm
  • Whom to contact, and any help offered
  • Individually, by secure means; no extra personal data in the notice itself

TIME: 2 minutes.

SAY: the notice to each person is plain words, sent individually by secure means, with one line on what they can do now, such as watching for fake RSSO messages. If individual notice is impossible, ask the NPC in writing to approve another way.

From first sign to lessons learned

A supervisor's account exported 900 applicant records, with ID numbers, at 2:00 AM. Nobody noticed until Monday.

The first hour

  • 9:00 Detect — the export is found. Declared Critical.
  • 9:05 Detect — ID numbers, so the clock starts at 9:00: reasonable belief, not certainty.
  • 9:10 Contain — sessions ended, password reset, MFA checked. Disconnect, do not power off.
  • 9:20 Preserve — logs copied, server imaged, chain of custody started.

Then, over the days

  • 9:45 Eradicate — access removed; admin passwords reset from a clean device.
  • Mon to Tue Recover — restored from a backup verified clean.
  • By Thu 9:00 Notify — the NPC and all 900 people. Full report by Saturday.
  • After Learn — MFA everywhere, leaver process, outbound alerts, restore tests.

  2. Timeline — discovery, declaration, each containment step, each notification, return to service
  3. Systems and data affected — kinds of data, how many, breach type
  4. Actions taken — containment, evidence, eradication, recovery
  5. Notification decisions — the NPC, the people, any request, DICT, police
  6. Draft notice to the NPC, and a plain-words notice to the people affected
  7. Preliminary root cause, linked to your Module 4 findings, and lessons learned with named owners and dates

What most teams get wrong

Starting the clock late

Waiting for certainty on Saturday afternoon. Smoke, not ashes.

Wiping before preserving

Cleaning or restoring before logs and images were kept.

A thin notice to the people

No content planned for applicants until Monday, or a notice with personal data in it.

No scribe

Decisions nobody recorded, so the report was rebuilt from memory.

Giving in to pressure

Agreeing to "say nothing was taken", or paying without legal advice and law enforcement.

Vendor shortcuts

Handing over the built-in admin password because service had to reopen.
